# SEO, AEO, GEO and Bots: Four Different Jobs, One Website

A business owner hears four labels and is told to “do all of them.”

SEO. AEO. GEO. Bots.

They show up in proposals, dashboards and conference talks as if they were one project with four names. They are related. They are not the same job, and buying them as four separate campaigns usually creates noise instead of visibility.

> SEO helps a page earn a place in search results. AEO helps a page become the direct answer. GEO helps a business be named when an AI writes the answer. Bots are the machines that fetch the page before any of that can happen.

A clinic, a trades company or a specialist consultant does not need four strategies that contradict each other. It needs one clear website, and a clear idea of which outcome each kind of visibility is supposed to produce.

## What each label is actually for

**SEO**, search engine optimization, is the work of helping search engines such as Google and Bing discover a page, understand its topic and consider it for a results page. The person still chooses a link. Teams usually talk about success as impressions, clicks and the queries where the business appears. No one outside the search engine can guarantee a ranking.

**AEO**, answer engine optimization, is the work of making one specific answer easy to lift out of a page. Featured snippets, “People also ask,” voice replies and other answer boxes want a short passage that stands on its own. A long page can rank well and still lose the answer box if the useful sentence is buried under a slogan.

**GEO**, generative engine optimization, is the work of making a business easy to describe accurately when a system writes a new answer instead of showing a list of links. Tools such as ChatGPT, Perplexity, Gemini, Copilot and Google’s AI Overviews may mention a business, cite a page or leave it out. A mention is not the same as a click, and an omission is not something a vendor can sell you out of.

**Bots** are not an optimization method. They are automated programs that request pages. Some are search crawlers. Some fetch a page to ground an answer someone just asked. Some collect text that may be used to train models. Some monitor uptime, audit sites or copy content. A line in an analytics report that says “bot” is not a customer.

## SEO: earn a place in the results

Search still starts with a question a person types or speaks. “Emergency electrician near me.” “Bookkeeping for a dental clinic.” “Workshop facilitator in Ottawa.”

Before a search engine can rank a page, it has to find the page, decide the page is eligible to be stored, and judge whether the page is a useful response to that question. Google describes this as crawling, indexing and serving. A polished homepage does not complete that sequence by itself.

For a small business, practical SEO is mostly specificity and access:

- one clear topic for each important page;
- titles and headings that name the service, not a slogan;
- ordinary HTML text, so facts are not trapped inside images;
- links between related services, locations and proof;
- a sitemap, working pages and addresses that stay stable;
- a consistent name, service area and contact path;
- evidence a buyer can check, such as qualifications, examples, policies and reviews you actually earned.

Local intent and specialist intent are different jobs. A plumber needs location and availability to be obvious. A compliance advisor needs depth on the problem. Copying one generic keyword list across both businesses makes each one harder to understand.

SEO does not require a new blog post every week. It requires the pages a real buyer would need, written so a search engine does not have to guess.

## AEO: become the answer, not only the link

Answer features are impatient. They look for a question and a direct reply.

“What should I bring to a first physiotherapy visit?” “How long does a typical furnace inspection take?” “Do you work with owner-managed companies, or only larger teams?”

A useful answer page states the reply in plain language near the question, then adds the detail a careful reader needs. A heading that says “Our approach,” followed by a paragraph of brand language, will not be selected as an answer even if the page ranks for a broader term.

AEO overlaps with SEO. The same page can earn a blue link and supply a snippet. The extra demand is that the answer can be extracted:

- the question is one your customers actually ask, in their words;
- the short answer stands on its own if it is read aloud;
- supporting detail comes after the answer, not instead of it;
- headings mark the question and the reply;
- any FAQ is visible on the page, not hidden in code.

Inventing questions nobody asks, stuffing markup with text visitors cannot see, or publishing dozens of thin Q&A pages does not make a business more answerable. It makes the site harder to trust.

## GEO: be easy to describe when the answer is written fresh

Generative systems do not stop at a list of links. They compose a response. Sometimes they cite sources. Sometimes they name a business without a link. Sometimes they blend several pages and get a detail wrong.

The job changes. You are no longer only trying to win a position. You are trying to be a source whose facts can be repeated without distortion.

The facts that matter are ordinary and specific:

- the trading name customers actually use;
- the services, in words a buyer would recognize;
- who the service is for, and who it is not for;
- where you work, including cities, regions or remote delivery when that is true;
- how a person reaches you;
- proof, such as named experience, published examples, credentials and policies.

If the homepage says “transformative solutions,” LinkedIn lists different service names, and the site never states the region, a person may still call. An assistant asked for a shortlist may choose the competitor whose offer can be explained in two sentences.

GEO is not a trick for forcing a chatbot to mention you. The platform decides what to retrieve, cite and generate. The practical work is to publish accurate, consistent, quotable information, then check from time to time whether public tools describe the business fairly. When they do not, correct the pages and profiles you control.

Google’s AI Overviews sit between AEO and GEO. They may lift a passage, synthesize several sources, or do both. Treating them as a separate product, detached from clear pages, usually wastes the budget.

## Bots: know which machines you are talking to

“Block the bots” and “let every AI bot in” are both incomplete advice.

A small business typically meets four kinds of automated visitors.

**Search crawlers**, such as Googlebot and Bingbot, fetch pages so a search engine can consider them. Blocking these removes the business from that search engine.

**Retrieval crawlers** request a page to ground an answer someone is asking now. Operators use names such as OAI-SearchBot, ChatGPT-User, Claude-SearchBot and PerplexityBot for this kind of fetch. Allowing them does not guarantee a citation. Blocking them can push that tool toward older copies or third-party descriptions of your business.

**Training crawlers**, such as GPTBot, ClaudeBot or Google-Extended, collect content that may be used to train or improve models. Allowing them is a policy choice about your writing. It is not an SEO tactic, and blocking them does not raise your rankings. Google-Extended is also separate from Googlebot: limiting model training does not, by itself, remove you from Google Search.

**Other automated traffic** includes uptime monitors, accessibility scanners, audit tools, scrapers and abusive bots. Some are useful. Some waste resources or copy your pages. None of them is a lead.

A robots.txt file is a public note about which automated visitors you welcome on which paths. It is not a lock. Well-behaved crawlers read it. Others ignore it. Private areas, such as a client hub, should stay out of public crawling no matter how you feel about AI.

Operators publish what they claim each crawler does, and those roles change. The useful habit is to read the current documentation when you set a policy, not to memorize one list from an article.

Analytics will count many of these requests. A spike in bot traffic is not a spike in demand. Filter it before you decide that a campaign worked or a page failed.

The decision worth writing down is simple:

- allow the search crawlers you want to be found by;
- decide whether training crawlers may use your public pages;
- keep retrieval access aligned with whether you want current pages available when someone asks an assistant about your field;
- keep private, personal and unpublished material out of reach;
- never treat a bot hit as a sales conversation.

## One page can do more than one job

Imagine a bookkeeping firm in Kingston. A page titled “Bookkeeping for dental clinics in Kingston” can do four things at once.

It can give a search engine a specific page to consider when someone searches that service and that place. It can open with a direct answer about what monthly bookkeeping includes, so an answer box has a passage to lift. It can state the firm name, the client type, the city and a concrete example, so a generative system can describe the firm without inventing a specialty. And it can be available to the crawlers the firm has chosen to allow.

Four outcomes. One page. The failure mode is a homepage that tries to sound clever to people and says almost nothing a machine can repeat, plus a set of profiles that contradict it.

## What to do first

Start with the work that helps every job, in this order:

1. Write the public facts in text: name, services, audiences, locations and a contact path.
2. Give each important service or customer question its own page, with a direct answer and proof.
3. Make those pages reachable by ordinary links, with a sitemap and addresses that do not change by accident.
4. Decide which bots are welcome, and keep private areas disallowed.
5. Make the website and the profiles you control say the same thing.
6. Read the signals separately: search impressions and clicks, the questions you can answer clearly, and an occasional check of how an AI tool describes you.

Decline offers that promise a chatbot ranking, a guaranteed citation, or a bot setting that replaces clear writing. Structured data can reinforce facts visitors can already see. It cannot rescue a vague page. Invented ratings or hidden answers create risk rather than visibility.

## Look at the whole path

Search engines and AI platforms control their own crawling, indexing, ranking, snippets and generated answers. Clearer signals make an accurate description more likely. They do not purchase a position, a featured answer or a mention.

If the pages are already specific and the business is still hard to find, the useful next step is one assessment of the whole path: technical access, content, local profiles, trust and the route from discovery to inquiry. That is one system. It is not four campaigns with four invoices.
