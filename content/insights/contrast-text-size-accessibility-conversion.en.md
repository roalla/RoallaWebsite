# Contrast and Text Size Are Conversion Tools—not Just Accessibility Features

When a website or app is difficult to read, the problem is not limited to accessibility.

Low-contrast text, undersized labels and weak visual hierarchy make every user work harder. Important details disappear. Calls to action compete with secondary content. Form instructions are missed. Prices, deadlines and error messages become easier to overlook.

That friction can affect comprehension, confidence and conversion long before someone identifies the experience as inaccessible.

> If users cannot quickly see what matters, they are less likely to understand the offer, trust the experience or complete the next step.

Contrast and text size are therefore both accessibility requirements and business design tools. Used well, they help people identify priorities, scan content, make decisions and act with less effort.

## Readability and attention are part of the same design problem

Accessibility conversations often begin with whether text can technically be read. Conversion conversations usually begin with whether a visitor notices the right message or button.

In practice, these questions are connected.

A person arriving on a service page needs to recognize, in order:

- what the organization offers;
- whether the offer is relevant;
- what evidence supports it;
- what action is available; and
- what will happen after that action.

Text size, weight, spacing, colour and contrast tell the eye where to begin and what to notice next. This is visual hierarchy.

When everything is large, bright or bold, nothing feels important. When everything is small and muted, important content disappears. Effective design creates meaningful differences while keeping every essential element readable.

## Contrast does more than make text legible

Contrast is the visible difference between an element and what surrounds it. It includes the relationship between text and its background, but also between buttons, fields, icons, borders, focus indicators and nearby content.

Strong contrast can help users:

- distinguish headings from supporting copy;
- recognize links and interactive controls;
- find the primary call to action;
- notice form errors and status messages;
- compare options and prices; and
- stay oriented while scanning a page.

That does not mean every conversion button should use the loudest colour available. A button succeeds when it is visually distinct, clearly labelled and placed where the user expects the next step.

Contrast should create hierarchy, not noise.

## Text size signals importance and reduces effort

Text size is often treated as a branding preference. It is more useful to treat it as part of the interface’s decision system.

Larger text can establish the main promise, separate sections and make a primary action easier to identify. Comfortable body text reduces the effort required to understand details. Clear labels and helper text prevent mistakes in forms and transactions.

Problems appear when:

- body copy is reduced to fit more content above the fold;
- mobile text is scaled down instead of reflowed;
- legal, pricing or eligibility details are visually minimized;
- form labels rely on tiny placeholder text;
- button labels are smaller than surrounding copy; or
- headings differ from paragraphs only by a slight weight change.

Small text may make a layout appear refined in a design file. On a real phone, in bright light, while moving or under time pressure, it can turn a simple task into a frustrating one.

## Better hierarchy can support conversion

Conversion depends on more than visual design. The offer must be relevant, the message credible and the requested action worthwhile.

Contrast and text size cannot repair a weak offer. They can prevent a strong offer from being hidden.

Good hierarchy supports conversion by helping users:

1. Find the value proposition without searching for it.
2. Separate proof and supporting detail from the main message.
3. Recognize which action is primary and which actions are optional.
4. Read instructions before making an error.
5. Confirm prices, terms and expectations before committing.
6. Recover quickly when a form or transaction needs correction.

Eye-tracking research on landing pages has connected clearer scanning patterns with more attention and click-throughs on calls to action. Other research warns that increasing visual intensity too far can create negative reactions faster than it improves conversion.

The practical lesson is balance: make the next important step easy to find without turning the interface into a collection of competing banners.

## What WCAG says about contrast

The Web Content Accessibility Guidelines, or WCAG, provide testable criteria for accessible digital experiences.

At Level AA, WCAG requires a contrast ratio of at least:

- **4.5:1 for normal text** against its background;
- **3:1 for large-scale text** against its background; and
- **3:1 for the visual information needed to identify many user-interface components and meaningful graphics** under WCAG 2.1 and later.

For the contrast exception, large-scale text generally means at least 18 point, approximately 24 CSS pixels, or at least 14 point bold, approximately 18.5 CSS pixels.

These ratios are minimums, not branding targets. Passing a ratio does not automatically make a design easy to understand. Thin type, text over photography, glare, screen quality and surrounding colours can still make content difficult to perceive.

Important information should also not depend on colour alone. A red border around an invalid field may be missed; a border plus an icon and a clear error message is more dependable.

## What WCAG says about text size and resizing

WCAG does not prescribe one universal minimum pixel size for body text.

Instead, it requires text to remain usable when people enlarge or adapt it. Under WCAG Level AA, text must be resizable up to 200 percent without losing content or functionality. Layouts should reflow rather than clip text, overlap controls or force impractical horizontal reading.

Modern WCAG guidance also expects interfaces to tolerate user-adjusted text spacing without losing content or functionality.

This makes relative sizing and responsive layout important. A design is not robust if it works only at one viewport width, browser zoom level or font setting.

For most business interfaces, a comfortable body-text baseline, clear heading scale and generous line height are safer starting points than trying to fit the maximum amount of copy into the smallest area. The final choice should be tested on real devices with real content and representative users.

## Ontario requirements under the AODA

In Ontario, the relevant law is the **Accessibility for Ontarians with Disabilities Act, 2005**, commonly called the AODA.

Under Ontario’s Integrated Accessibility Standards Regulation, designated public-sector organizations and businesses or non-profits with 50 or more employees must make their public websites and web content published after January 1, 2012 conform to WCAG 2.0 Level AA, with stated exceptions for live captions and pre-recorded audio descriptions.

Organizations with fewer than 50 employees are not subject to that specific website-conformance requirement under the regulation, although other accessibility duties may still apply and accessible design remains sound business practice.

The legal requirement is WCAG 2.0 AA, but WCAG has continued to evolve. Designing toward WCAG 2.2 AA can address newer interaction patterns and reduce the risk of building to an older minimum that will require remediation later.

This article provides general information, not legal advice. Organizations should confirm which requirements apply to their size, sector, content and jurisdiction.

## Beyond Ontario: WCAG is widely used as a digital benchmark

WCAG is used well beyond Ontario. Governments, regulated sectors, procurement programs and organizations in many jurisdictions reference versions of WCAG in laws, policies, contracts and digital standards.

The exact obligation varies. The applicable WCAG version, conformance level, organization size, sector, enforcement process and exceptions may differ by jurisdiction.

For organizations serving customers across regions, treating accessibility as a core product-quality requirement is more resilient than maintaining separate low-standard and high-standard experiences.

## Common design mistakes that hurt both access and conversion

### Muted text everywhere

Light grey can work for genuinely secondary information. When it is used for body copy, labels, prices and instructions, the interface loses both readability and hierarchy.

### Tiny text used to make the design feel premium

Small typography can look spacious in a mockup while making the live experience slower and less trustworthy.

### A call to action that blends into the page

A brand-consistent button can still be too similar to its background or surrounding elements. The primary action should be recognizable without making users hunt for it.

### Too many competing accents

If every card, badge and button uses a saturated colour, the interface has contrast but no priority.

### Text placed directly over busy images

A contrast check performed against one part of an image may pass while the text fails over another. A solid overlay, controlled image treatment or separate text area is more reliable.

### Important details visually treated as disclaimers

Eligibility, price, renewal terms, privacy choices and form requirements should be readable before commitment. Hiding essential information in tiny or faint text increases risk and weakens trust.

### Desktop-first typography

A hierarchy that works on a large monitor may collapse on mobile. Line length, wrapping, zoom, touch targets and screen glare change the experience.

## A practical review for websites and apps

Review the most important customer journeys rather than testing only the home page.

1. Identify the primary message and action on each screen.
2. Check text and component contrast with a reliable measurement tool.
3. Test at 200 percent zoom and with increased text spacing.
4. Review mobile layouts on real devices and in different lighting.
5. Confirm that labels, instructions and errors remain visible and understandable.
6. Navigate with a keyboard and verify visible focus indicators.
7. Test the journey with people, including users with disabilities.
8. Compare completion, error and abandonment data before and after changes.

Automated tools can identify many contrast failures. They cannot decide whether the visual hierarchy communicates the right business priority or whether the wording makes an action worth taking.

## Design for clarity, then measure the result

Accessibility and conversion should not be treated as competing objectives.

Both depend on reducing unnecessary effort. People need to see the message, understand the choice and recognize the next step.

Use WCAG as the minimum testable foundation. Use research, analytics and user testing to determine whether the design also directs attention effectively and helps people complete meaningful tasks.

> The goal is not to make everything louder. It is to make the right information unmistakable.
